## Summary

- **Linear static stress analysis runs in the browser, accurately and fast, with no server.** The pipeline: the kernel writes the body as STEP (as `exportStep` does today), gmsh compiled to WebAssembly meshes it into quadratic tetrahedra (TET10) in a worker, and a TypeScript solver assembles the stiffness matrix, solves it with conjugate gradients preconditioned by smoothed-aggregation algebraic multigrid (AMG), and recovers nodal stresses. It ran unchanged in Node, Chromium 153, Firefox 155 and WebKit 26.6, and the peak von Mises stress of every scaling run agrees across all four to the fourth decimal.
- **Mesher: gmsh, from the prebuilt npm package `@loumalouomega/gmsh-wasm` 0.3.0 (GPL-2.0-or-later).** It includes gmsh's OpenCASCADE kernel, so it imports the kernel's STEP directly, keeps curved faces exact (mid-side nodes on the true surface) and keeps the kernel's face order (all 31 faces of the 4 solids matched by centre). It meshed about 500k degrees of freedom (DOF) in 2.0 to 3.4 s. No mesher had to be built: there is no Netgen or TetGen build to WebAssembly on npm, TetGen is AGPL and excluded anyway, and gmsh made both a Netgen build and an own fallback mesher unnecessary. fTetWild (`float-tetwild-wasm`, MPL-2.0) also works, but meshes 5 to 20 times slower and only approximates the boundary; it is the fallback for broken imported geometry.
- **Solver: our own TypeScript, TET10 with AMG-preconditioned conjugate gradients.** AMG converged in 17 to 26 iterations at every size from 3k to 940k DOF. The one-level preconditioners grow with the mesh: IC(0) needed 342 iterations at 20k DOF and 1,073 at 500k; Jacobi 2,881 at 118k. At 500k DOF AMG is 3.6 to 4.5 times faster than IC(0) in total time. CalculiX was not needed and was not built.
- **Accuracy: every metric of every benchmark at every density is within 1.4 % of the analytical value** (cantilever against Timoshenko beam theory, plate with a hole against Peterson's Kt, thick-walled cylinder against Lame), most within 0.8 %, well inside the 5 % target. The coarsest meshes (3k to 8k DOF) already meet it.
- **Time, one analysis from STEP bytes to nodal stresses, AMG:** about 50k DOF 1.0 to 1.4 s, about 200k DOF 3.7 to 6.2 s, about 500k DOF 10.2 to 16.0 s across the three browsers (WebKit fastest, Firefox slowest), on a desktop Ryzen 5 7600X. 200k DOF is far inside 30 s, so no smaller limit is needed. One run at 940k DOF in Chromium took 28.7 s.
- **Memory is the limit, not time.** The solver's own arrays are 283 MiB at 200k DOF and 696 MiB at 500k (about 1.4 KiB per DOF); the browser's renderer peaked at 0.85 to 1.3 GiB at 200k, 1.6 to 2.4 GiB at 500k, and 3.0 GiB at 940k. gmsh's wasm memory stays at its initial 64 MiB up to 500k DOF.
- **Recommended limit:** a hard cap of **500k DOF**, a default mesh aimed at about **150k to 200k DOF**, and a warning above 200k that the run may take tens of seconds and gigabytes on a slower machine. On a laptop 2 to 3 times slower than this desktop (estimate), 200k DOF would take about 8 to 18 s and 500k about 20 to 48 s.
- **Licences: allowed, with two actions.** gmsh-wasm is GPL-2.0-or-later and our solver is our own GPL code. But the prebuilt `.wasm` statically contains OCCT (LGPL-2.1 with the Open CASCADE exception) and a dozen gmsh contrib libraries, one of which, Blossom IV/Concorde, is used under a special permission for use within Gmsh rather than a GPL-compatible licence. So: ship a gmsh `.wasm` **built in this repository with Blossom and the unneeded contribs switched off** (the package's build scripts take the switches), and **amend ADR 0006** to admit gmsh-wasm and the LGPL components inside it as a separate, replaceable module. See [Licences](#licences).
- **Download:** the gmsh `.wasm` is 41.4 MB, 11.6 MB gzipped, 7.8 MB with Brotli; loaded only when the user first runs an analysis. It contains a second copy of OCCT; a build without OCC (about 12 MB per the package's README) fed with the kernel's triangulation is the way to shrink it later (not measured).

## The pipeline

1. **Geometry.** `geometry.ts` builds each solid with the kernel (`box`, `cylinder`, `boolean`) and writes it with `exportStep`, which is how a body would leave the regen worker for an FEA worker (plain bytes, ADR 0007).
2. **Mesh.** `mesh.ts` writes the STEP into gmsh's in-memory file system, imports it with gmsh's OCC kernel, sets the element size (`Mesh.MeshSizeMax`, optionally a distance field for local refinement) and `Mesh.ElementOrder = 2`, generates the 3D mesh and reads the nodes and the 10-node tetrahedra. gmsh's edge-node order is mapped to ours geometrically (each edge node is nearest its own corner pair's midpoint) rather than taken on trust from documentation; the nodes are then renumbered with reverse Cuthill-McKee.
3. **Constraints and loads.** Homogeneous displacement constraints by node predicate; uniform tractions and pressures on boundary faces whose three corners pass a predicate, integrated over each curved 6-node face with a 6-point rule, so a pressure follows the true normal of a curved face. Each run checks the applied total force (for example exactly 10,000 N per axis on the cylinder quarter).
4. **Assembly.** Isotropic linear elastic TET10 elements, 4-point Gauss rule, assembled into the upper triangle of 3x3 node blocks (`solver.ts`).
5. **Solve.** Conjugate gradients to a relative residual of 1e-8, preconditioned by smoothed-aggregation AMG (`amg.ts`): the six rigid body modes as near-null space (zeroed on constrained components), Vanek's three-pass aggregation on block strength (threshold 0.02), the tentative prolongator by a QR per aggregate, one damped-Jacobi smoothing step of the prolongator, Galerkin coarse matrices with 6x6 blocks, a symmetric V-cycle with degree-2 Chebyshev smoothing, and a dense Cholesky solve on the coarsest level (at most 900 DOF). Three or four levels result.
6. **Stress.** Stress evaluated at each element's ten nodes and averaged per node; von Mises from it.

The same `run.ts` runs in Node and in the browser worker (`src/browser/worker.ts`), which loads gmsh's files as they are (not bundled) from the page's origin.

### DOF limit

200k DOF finished in 3.7 to 6.2 s in every browser on this machine, far inside the 30 s threshold, so the plan's fallback (a smaller model limit) is not needed for time. The limit comes from memory and from slower machines:

- **Hard cap: 500k DOF.** 10 to 16 s here; 1.6 to 2.4 GiB of renderer memory. At 940k DOF the renderer reached 3.0 GiB, close to what a browser tab may hold on a typical 8 to 16 GiB laptop, and 28.7 s even on this desktop.
- **Default mesh: about 150k to 200k DOF**, the size at which every benchmark is already within 1 %, sized by an element length derived from the body's dimensions and refined at holes and fillets.
- **Warn above 200k DOF** that the run may take tens of seconds and a few gigabytes. Show progress per phase (mesh, setup, iterations), and allow cancelling between phases and between iterations.
- The estimate for a laptop 2 to 3 times slower than this desktop: 200k DOF 8 to 18 s, 500k DOF 20 to 48 s. "We can make it faster later" has clear first steps: HXT meshing (halves meshing), running the conjugate gradient's sparse products on several workers over `SharedArrayBuffer` (the app is already cross-origin isolated), and the solver kernels in WebAssembly with SIMD.

## Face identity across the hand-over

Loads and constraints in M9 are attached to faces (decision 3). `faces.json`: for each of the four solids, gmsh's surface tag n after importing the kernel's STEP is the kernel's face n, matched by centre of area (all 6, 7, 6 and 12 faces, worst distance 0.0009 mm). So T9.6 can map a face reference to gmsh's surface tag directly and read that surface's 6-node triangles (`getElementsByType(9, tag)`) to apply a load, instead of the spike's coordinate predicates. This held for these solids; T9.6a should keep a test that checks it on a multi-body document and fail loudly if the counts differ.

## Risks

- **Licence of the prebuilt mesher.** As above: shipping the npm package as it is would ship Blossom. Mitigation: our own build (action 1), and the licence check (`pnpm licenses:check`) extended to cover the gmsh build's components.
- **Download size.** 7.8 MB (Brotli) on the first analysis, on top of libcascade. Mitigation: load it only for FEA, show download progress, cache it; later, a gmsh build without OCC meshing the kernel's own triangulation (`classifySurfaces` and `createGeometry`; about 12 MB raw per the package's README), at the cost of approximate curved surfaces (not measured).
- **Memory on small machines.** 500k DOF peaked at 1.6 to 2.4 GiB. A tab that runs out of memory dies without an error the app can catch. Mitigation: the 500k cap, a default near 200k, and a worker of its own so the crash takes only the analysis.
- **Mesh failures on real geometry.** Only clean kernel solids were meshed. Thin walls, sliver faces and tangent fillets can make gmsh fail or produce poor elements. Mitigation in T9.6: report gmsh's errors, check element quality (the solver already refuses inverted elements), retry with HXT or a smaller size near small features, and offer fTetWild as the fallback.
- **Face mapping.** Held on four solids; not proven for compounds, multi-body STEP or bodies whose topology gmsh's import heals. Keep a test.
- **Element locking and stress singularities.** TET10 does not lock in bending or for nu = 0.3, but stresses at re-entrant corners and point constraints grow without bound as the mesh refines. The results UI must say so, and calc records should report stress away from constraints or flag singular points.
- **Isotropic linear elasticity only.** Wood (orthotropic), plastics' nonlinearity, contact and large deflection are out of scope; the calc record must name the assumption (decision 1).
- **Timing noise.** The machine was shared with other agents; at a load average of 16 the same solve took about twice as long. The numbers here are from load averages below 2.
- **Garbage in the browsers.** Memory peaks include garbage from earlier runs in the same page; the 940k run in a fresh page is the clean upper data point.

## Recommendations

1. **Browser only, in a worker of its own: gmsh (our build) for meshing, our TypeScript TET10 solver with AMG-preconditioned conjugate gradients for solving**, exactly the pipeline of this spike. No server fallback.
2. **Limits:** cap at 500k DOF; default mesh aimed at 150k to 200k DOF; warn above 200k. Revisit when the solver is multi-threaded.
3. **T9.6a** should take over `tet10.ts`, `solver.ts` (assembly, loads, stresses), `amg.ts` and `order.ts` as a package (`packages/fea` or inside `packages/calc`), with the three benchmarks as tests, and add: face-based loads and constraints through gmsh's surface tags, bolt and bearing loads as distributed tractions, HXT as the default 3D algorithm, element-quality checks, cancellation, progress reporting, and memory release between phases.
4. **ADR 0017** should record the choice and the cap; the ADR 0006 amendment and the gmsh build recipe come with T9.6a, before anything ships.

The browser probe needs the three Playwright browsers and, without root, the unpacked libraries described there.
